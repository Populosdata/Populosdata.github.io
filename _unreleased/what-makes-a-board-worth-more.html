Same street, different value

Put two billboards on the same stretch of road, carrying the same traffic past them every day, and they can still be worth strikingly different amounts to an advertiser. One faces the traffic squarely, sits at a legible height, is clean and well-lit, and stands alone. The other is angled awkwardly, partly hidden by a tree, faded, and crowded by three competing boards. The road is identical; the audience each one truly delivers is not.

Rating a board is rating its ability to convert traffic into views

A board rating is a structured attempt to score exactly this: how effectively a given structure turns the traffic passing it into genuine views. It is the operational form of the visibility principle instead of a vague sense that one board is 'better', it produces a defensible number that can scale the board's audience up or down and feed directly into pricing and planning.

The factors that make up the score

A robust rating is composite, built from factors that each independently affect real visibility:

Where it sits

  • Situation: whether it sits at an intersection, above the road, or simply roadside, which changes how naturally it falls into view.
  • Run-up: how far away the board becomes visible as you approach, giving the eye time to find and read it.
  • Angle: how squarely the board faces the flow of traffic.

What is around it

  • Visibility and obstruction: whether anything physically blocks the sightline.
  • Competitive environment: whether it stands alone or competes for attention with a cluster of neighbours.

The board itself

  • Size: larger faces are legible from further away and for longer.
  • Condition: a torn, faded or damaged board loses the audience even in a perfect position.
  • Illumination: whether and how well it is lit, which governs its night-time audience.

Field truth plus machine verification

Some of these factors are stable and can be measured once: size, run-up, basic situation. Others are volatile and must be checked repeatedly, because they change: condition degrades, a new board appears next door, a light stops working. The strongest approach combines trained field observation with computer-vision verification, so that a human judgement about condition or obstruction is cross-checked by an image-based system rather than taken on trust. The rule that keeps this honest is simple: the machine flags and recommends, a human decides, and every change is logged.

One score, used once

The discipline in wiring a rating into a measurement system is to make sure it enters the audience calculation exactly once. Siting quality should scale the impression at source; it must not also be applied a second time somewhere downstream, or the same advantage gets counted twice and every derived number inflates. A rating is only trustworthy if a buyer can see that it has been applied cleanly and consistently.

How Populos scores a board

Populos rates every board on a single rating assembled from exactly these factors, each with transparent, defined scoring bands, versioned and signed off, rather than buried in code. Stable factors are captured once; volatile ones like condition, competitive clutter are re-checked on every field visit and verified by computer vision. That single rating is applied once, never twice. The outcome is that a board's price and its planned audience both reflect what it can actually do, and two boards on the same road are valued for what they genuinely deliver.

Why buyers and media owners both win

A transparent rating aligns the market. Buyers stop overpaying for well-located but poorly sited inventory. Media owners who invest in good positioning, maintenance and illumination are rewarded with higher-rated, higher-value boards. And the whole market gains a shared, defensible language for why one board costs more than its neighbour; which is exactly what an independent measurement currency is for.
