Planners buy audiences, not spaces

The modern media buyer does not just want to buy a billboard; they want to buy a route to a particular kind of person. That shift, from buying space to buying audiences, is what pushes OOH measurement past the head-count into the harder question of who is passing a board, not merely how many.

What audience profiling is trying to do

Audience profiling estimates the composition of the people exposed to a structure: broad characteristics such as age, and most valuable in a commercial context, socio-economic profile. A brand selling premium goods and a brand selling everyday essentials may both want a busy corridor, but they want to know whether the crowd on that corridor skews toward their customer. Profiling is what lets a plan be judged on relevance, not just reach.

The honest problem: you cannot survey a billboard's audience directly

Here lies the central challenge. You cannot stop and interview everyone who passes a board. In markets with decades of continuous passive travel panels, profiles are inferred from those panels. In a market without them, the profile has to be built by inference from the best available population data and that inference must be done carefully and stated honestly, while backing it up with sample audience surveys, or it becomes fiction dressed as data.

Building a profile from the ground up

A responsible socio-economic model in a data-scarce market is built from official, representative sources rather than assumption. Census microdata on household assets, housing quality and sanitation can be used to derive a socio-economic classification at a fine geographic grain. Living-standards surveys and syndicated audience data can calibrate and sanity-check the bands. The socio-economic character of a location is then assembled from the characteristics of the communities that actually feed movement onto that road not from a guess about the neighbourhood it happens to sit in.

The methodological discipline that matters most here is keeping the profile separate from the count. A socio-economic profile describes the composition of an audience; it must never be quietly multiplied into the audience size to inflate it. The number of people and the kind of people are two different attributes, and a credible system reports them side by side rather than blending them into a single flattering figure.

Refreshing, versioning and admitting uncertainty

Socio-economic reality changes, and a profile is only as good as its inputs. Good practice is to version the model, recomputing composition as new data arrives, keeping old versions on record, and signing off changes. Equally important is labelling confidence: a profile derived from strong local data deserves more weight than one leaning on triangulation and proxies, and a buyer is entitled to know which they are getting.

The Populos approach

Populos builds its socio-economic classification from Ghana's own census microdata and living-standards surveys, calibrated against syndicated audience measures. A road's profile is derived from the communities that feed traffic onto it through the network, rather than by assuming the character of the immediate surroundings. Socio-economic share sits alongside the impression count as a separate audience attribute, never multiplied into it, and the model is versioned and reviewed on a set cadence, with each revision signed off and logged. Where a value rests on proxy or triangulation rather than hard local data, it is treated as provisional until field evidence confirms it.

Why the restraint is the point

It would be easy to present socio-economic profiles with a confident single decimal and let buyers assume a precision the underlying data cannot support. The more valuable and more defensible path is to build the profile from real national data, keep it honest about its own uncertainty, and never let it inflate the head-count. An audience profile a buyer can trust is worth more than one that merely flatters the plan.
