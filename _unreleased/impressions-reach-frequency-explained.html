Four words that do most of the work

Almost every out-of-home media plan, anywhere in the world, is assembled from four core measures: impressions, reach, frequency and gross rating points. Understanding what each genuinely represents and what it does not is the difference between buying audiences and buying guesswork.

Impressions: scale, not impact

An impression is one likely view of an advertisement by one person. In OOH it is estimated, not clicked: it is built from traffic past a structure, adjusted for how visible that structure realistically is. A board on a busy corridor might generate several million impressions across a campaign month.

The honest limitation of impressions is that they measure scale and nothing more. They tell you how big the exposure was, not whether it moved anyone. A large impressions number on a poorly sited board is still a large number of low-quality exposures. This is why serious measurement systems weight impressions by a quality factor rather than reporting raw traffic. A board's siting quality should raise or lower the audience it is credited with.

Reach: the unduplicated crowd

Reach is the number of distinct individuals exposed at least once, usually expressed as a percentage of a target population. The crucial word is distinct. A campaign can generate millions of impressions but reach only a large share of the audience, because many of those people pass the same board repeatedly on their daily commute.

Reach is where naive counting does the most damage. If you add up the audiences of ten boards along one commuter corridor, you will count the same commuters ten times over. Credible reach requires deduplication; recognizing that the same person seen on Monday and again on Tuesday, or seen on two nearby boards, is one person reached, not several. Systems that skip this step inflate dramatically.

Frequency: how often the average person saw it

Frequency is the average number of times a reached person was exposed. Reach and frequency are two halves of the same total: for a fixed weight of advertising, pushing reach up tends to pull frequency down, and vice versa. Decades of advertising research suggest a window of roughly three to five exposures tends to build recall efficiently without wasting spend on over-exposure; though the right number depends on the brand, the message and the market.

GRPs: one number to weigh the campaign

Gross rating points combine the two: reach multiplied by frequency. A campaign reaching half of its target audience an average of four times delivers 200 GRPs. GRPs are useful as a single measure of campaign weight and for comparing plans of different shapes. But they are a blunt instrument: 200 GRPs concentrated on a narrow audience seen many times is a very different campaign from 200 GRPs spread thinly across a wide one, even though the headline number is identical.

The trap: confusing the big number with the good number

The recurring mistake in OOH planning is treating the largest number as the best outcome. Impressions are the easiest metric to inflate and the least informative about impact. A disciplined plan reads all four measures together: impressions for scale, reach for how much of the audience was touched, frequency for how often, and GRPs for overall weight: always with deduplication applied and siting quality baked into the impression itself.

How Populos treats these numbers

In the Populos model these are not four independent counts but one coherent chain. Impressions are quality-adjusted at source, so a board's visibility, angle, size and condition shape the audience it earns. Reach is deduplicated across days and across nearby boards on the same corridor and campaign. Frequency falls out of the relationship between quality-adjusted impressions and deduplicated reach. The aim is a plan where every number means what a buyer thinks it means.
