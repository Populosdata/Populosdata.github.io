A model built for the wrong city

Most mature OOH measurement systems were designed around private-vehicle movement on well-instrumented road networks, supplemented by rail and metro flows. That design reflects the cities that built them. Transplanted unchanged to an African metropolis, it systematically mis-measures the audience, because it under-weights the two things that dominate exposure here: people on foot, and people moving through informal shared transport.

The pedestrian audience is not a rounding error

In and around Ghanaian markets, lorry stations, transit hubs and high-street corridors, pedestrian volumes are not a minor supplement to vehicle traffic, they are frequently the main event. A board overlooking a busy market frontage may earn most of its real audience from people on foot who dwell, browse and linger, giving far more genuine viewing time than a passing vehicle ever could.

Pedestrian exposure also behaves differently. Walkers move slowly, change direction, stop and cluster. Their dwell time near a structure can be many times that of a vehicle occupant, which changes both how many see a board and how well they absorb it. A measurement system that treats a pedestrian pass-by as equivalent to a vehicle pass-by gets both the count and the quality wrong.

Trotros, shared taxis and the informal network

Ghana's transport backbone is substantially informal: trotros and shared taxis running semi-fixed routes, concentrating at terminals and stops. These vehicles carry high passenger loads at low speeds through dense corridors, and they pool at predictable points. That pooling creates exposure hotspots terminals and stops where large numbers of people wait, board and alight; that a private-vehicle model would never flag as significant.

Capturing this requires occupancy assumptions suited to shared transport rather than private cars, and it requires treating terminals and stops as audience generators in their own right. The number of people a corridor delivers depends heavily on how full the vehicles are and how the informal network feeds them (inputs that must come from local transport studies, not global defaults).

Transit points as structured audience generators

Around a market, station or terminal, the audience is not spread evenly along a road. It concentrates around the point itself and decays with distance. Modelling this properly means treating each significant point of interest as a source of foot traffic, estimating how many daily trips it generates, what share of those become walking journeys past a given board, and how that flow thins out as you move away from the point. A board's pedestrian audience is then the sum of the flows from the generators within realistic walking distance, plus the through-movement of people simply passing along the corridor.

How Populos captures the real street

Populos models pedestrian traffic intensity and vehicular traffic intensity as distinct streams, each calibrated to Ghanaian data. Pedestrian audiences around transit points are built up from the trip-generating power of nearby markets, stations, terminals, schools and places of worship, with realistic walking-distance catchments and distance decay. Vehicular audiences use occupancy assumptions appropriate to shared informal transport, calibrated against local traffic-count and transport-study data. The intent is a measurement of the street as it actually is in Accra, Kumasi, Takoradi and other capital cities in Ghana, not as it would be in a city built around the private car.

The pay-off: fairness and discovery

Measuring pedestrian and informal-transport audiences properly does two things. It makes the numbers fair (inventory that serves foot and transit audiences finally gets credited for the audience it truly delivers). And it surfaces value that a vehicle-only model hides: high-performing sites around markets and terminals that would otherwise be invisible on a plan. In an African city, this is not a refinement. It is the core of getting the measurement right at all.
