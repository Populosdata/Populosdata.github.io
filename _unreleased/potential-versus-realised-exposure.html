The most expensive assumption in OOH

The most expensive assumption a measurement system can make is that everyone who passes a board sees it. It is also the easiest assumption to make, because raw traffic data is cheap and visibility science is hard. A system that skips the visibility step is not measuring an audience; it is reporting a traffic count with an advertising label attached.

Opportunity to contact versus visibility-adjusted contact

International practice draws a firm line between two quantities. The opportunity-to-contact figure is the raw count of people who passed within some notional range of a structure. The visibility-adjusted figure is the smaller, more honest number: those whose realistic sightline, direction of travel and available viewing time meant they genuinely had a chance to take the advertisement in. The leading currencies built their credibility precisely by making this adjustment rigorously, using eye-tracking research to understand how people actually distribute their attention in a cluttered environment.

What makes a pass-by into a view

Whether a passing person becomes a genuine viewer depends on a stack of physical factors, each measurable:

  • Angle: a board perpendicular to the flow of traffic is seen far more readily than one set at a shallow, glancing angle.
  • Size and elevation: dimensions and height above the ground change how far away a board becomes legible and for how long it stays in view.
  • Obstruction: trees, poles, other boards and buildings routinely block sightlines that a map would suggest are clear.
  • Dwell and speed: a pedestrian dawdling near a market has far more viewing time than a driver at speed; the same board delivers different real exposure to each.
  • Illumination and condition: an unlit board at night, or a torn and faded one by day, delivers a fraction of its notional audience.

Turning visibility into a single, fair adjustment

The discipline is to combine these factors into one defensible quality score for each structure, and then to let that score scale the audience the board is credited with. A board that scores highly on siting keeps most of its raw traffic as real audience; a poorly sited board rightly loses much of it. Crucially, this quality factor must enter the calculation once and cleanly. It should adjust the impression at source rather than being applied twice through different doors, which would distort every downstream number.

The Populos approach: one entry point for quality

Populos scores every board on a single rating built from field-verified factors of run-up, situation, visibility, angle, size, condition, illumination and the competitive clutter around it. That single rating is the one place siting quality enters the audience calculation. A board's raw traffic intensity is converted into an 'Eyes On' audience by applying its rating, consistent with the visibility-adjustment principle used by the established global currencies. The result is that two boards on the same road with the same traffic can, correctly, be credited with very different real audiences.

Why this matters to a buyer

For an advertiser, the visibility adjustment is what protects the budget. It is the mechanism that stops a well-connected but badly sited board from being sold on the strength of a big traffic number. It rewards media owners who maintain and position their inventory well, and it gives buyers a number that reflects what their campaign actually achieved rather than what the geography theoretically allowed.
